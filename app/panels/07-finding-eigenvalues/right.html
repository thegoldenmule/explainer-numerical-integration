Under-, critically, and overdamped

Sweep the damping and the discriminant passes through zero. The two roots slide along the real axis, collide at −c/2m, and split up and down into the plane. Beside them the closed-form solution changes shape. Drag the numbers:

c2−4mk= (0.10)2 −4·1.00 ·100.0 =−399.9

Right now that is negative, so the spring is underdamped, with roots λ1=… and λ2=….

Overdamped, c2>4mk: two real roots r1, r2, and the mass creeps home without crossing zero, x=Aer1t+Ber2t.

Critically damped is the single value of c in between. Set the discriminant to zero, c2−4mk=0, and solve: c=2mk=20.00. There the two roots collide on the real axis into one repeated root at −c/2m, and it is the exact boundary: any less damping and they are a complex pair and the mass rings; any more and they are two real roots and it does not. It is also the fastest return with no overshoot, which is why a camera or a suspension is tuned to sit near it. A drag will never land on it exactly — the button beside the plane sets it.

Underdamped, c2<4mk: a conjugate pair α±iω, and the mass rings inside a decaying envelope:

x=eαt (x0cosωt+ v0−αx0ω sinωt)

with α=−c/2m=−0.050. The real part of λ is the decay rate; the imaginary part is the ring frequency. Both games and springs live here: the demo spring has c=0.10 against a critical 2mk=20.00, barely damped at all.

The damping you sweep here is this pane’s own copy, 0.10: an overdamped view is a what-if, so it is never written back. The spring the rest of the piece integrates keeps c=0.10. m and k are the real spring’s, and dragging them does change it.
