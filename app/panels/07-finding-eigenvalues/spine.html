Finding eigenvalues

Now let’s add physics back in. What we’re actually doing is isolating the scaling characteristic of our force equation, and measuring how big it is. Mathematically, this means we need to solve for our eigenvalues λi when p(λ)=mλ2+cλ+k=0. You actually know how to find these roots. It’s the one equation you remember from high school: the quadratic equation, λ=(−c±c2−4mk)/2m. Drag the numbers:

λ= −(0.10) ± (0.10)2 −4·1.00·100.0 2·1.00

The discriminant c2−4mk=−399.99 is negative, so the system is underdamped and the roots are λ1=…, λ2=…, two points in the complex plane.

The precise definition of physical stability is simple: “If all eigenvalues have negative real parts, the system is stable. If at least one eigenvalue has positive real parts, the system is unstable.” (Eberly). If and only if this is true, our solution is going to stick close to the explicit solution. Both roots here have real part −c2m=−0.050, which is negative: every mode decays and the spring settles.

“If we choose force parameters that, downstream, result in eigenvalues with real parts greater than 0, we cannot guarantee stability of our physics engine.” Drag c below zero and watch it happen: negative damping is a force that pumps energy in instead of taking it out, both roots slide into the shaded right half-plane, and the oscillation grows without bound. Nothing downstream — no integrator, no step size — can save an engine whose physics is already doing that.

This is a local what-if. The c you drag here is this pane’s own copy, 0.10, with its own range down to −5; the spring the rest of the piece integrates keeps c=0.10. m and k are the real spring’s, and dragging them does change it.
