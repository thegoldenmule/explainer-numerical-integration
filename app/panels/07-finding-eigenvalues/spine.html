Finding eigenvalues

Now let’s add physics back in. What we’re actually doing is isolating the scaling characteristic of our force equation, and measuring how big it is. Mathematically, this means we need to solve for our eigenvalues λi when p(λ)=mλ2+cλ+k=0. You actually know how to find these roots. It’s the one equation you remember from high school: the quadratic equation! With the current sliders:

λ= −0.10 ± 0.102 −4·1.00·100.0 2·1.00

The discriminant c2−4mk=−399.99 is negative, so the system is underdamped and the roots are λ1=…, λ2=…, two points in the complex plane.

The precise definition of physical stability is simple: “If all eigenvalues have negative real parts, the system is stable. If at least one eigenvalue has positive real parts, the system is unstable.” (Eberly). If and only if this is true, our solution is going to stick close to the explicit solution. Both roots here have real part −c2m=−0.050, which is negative: the spring settles. Choose force parameters that push a real part above zero and nothing downstream can save the engine.
