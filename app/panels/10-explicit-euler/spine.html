Runge-Kutta family: explicit Euler

The most common class of methods used in rigid body physics engines are Runge-Kutta methods. This class includes the oft-used explicit Euler method:

xi+1=xi+h·f(ti,xi)

The h is our time step, or dt. Apply the method to the modal equation, f=λx, and the error ratio has a single root, z=1+hλ (the derivation is the left pane). Here we introduce the definition of numerical stability:

Euler’s method is stable if and only if

|z|=|1+hλ|≤1

Since z is a ratio of future to present errors, a value greater than 1 would mean that error is growing. Shaded, that inequality is a disk in the complex plane, and the eigenvalues from panel 7 sit on it in green or red. For the current spring at h=0.033, |1+hλ|=1.0525, outside the disk: unstable — error doubles every 2.3 s.

Graham Rhodes gives an intuitive reading of this graph: the imaginary axis represents oscillation while the real axis shows growth or decay. So Euler’s method needs aggressive dampening and is unstable under oscillation, like what might happen with a spring force.
