Eigen-what-now

Forget all the physics for a second.

Say you have a matrix, A. If you were to multiply A by some vector, you would end up with a transformed version of it. An eigenvector (we’re not to eigenvalues yet) defines a direction that is invariant under that transformation, except for scaling.

This means that, regardless of the crazy transformation that A might apply (maybe it skews and rotates), an eigenvector gives you an arrow in space such that A only changes it in length. It’s an arrow that only grows and shrinks under the transformation.

Av=λv

An eigenvalue is the magnitude of that scaling transformation, like 3 or 12 or π. It’s just a number that represents how far the matrix stretched or squished that invariant vector.

The matrix here is the spring’s own, built from the current m and k with a damping c of this panel’s own — the spring’s real c is left alone. Drag v around the plane. R=A^v is drawn split: the part of v that lies along an invariant direction, scaled by that direction’s eigenvalue, plus what is left over. Land on a direction and the leftover vanishes and R=λv. Damp lightly and no real direction survives: the eigenvalues have gone complex, which is the next panel.
